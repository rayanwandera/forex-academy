MODULE 01

What Is Forex?

Forex is short for Foreign Exchange — the market where currencies are exchanged. Imagine someone traveling from Uganda to the US exchanging Ugandan Shillings for US Dollars. In Forex trading, traders speculate on changes in the value of one currency relative to another.

Currency pairs

Currencies are quoted in pairs, e.g. EUR/USD. The first currency (EUR) is the base currency; the second (USD) is the quote currency.

        EUR / USD
         │    │
         │    └── Quote Currency (US Dollar)
         │
         └─────── Base Currency (Euro)

What EUR/USD = 1.1000 means

Conceptually, 1 Euro = 1.10 US Dollars. If price rises from 1.1000 → 1.1050, the Euro has strengthened relative to the Dollar over that move. If it falls to 1.0950, the Euro has weakened.

Buying vs selling a pair

Buy EUR/USD — you're generally expecting the price to rise (entry 1.1000 → price rises to 1.1050). Sell EUR/USD — you're generally expecting the price to fall (entry 1.1000 → price falls to 1.0950). The actual result depends on execution price and trading costs.

Major, minor and exotic pairs

Majors are heavily traded and usually include USD: EUR/USD, GBP/USD, USD/JPY, USD/CHF, AUD/USD, USD/CAD, NZD/USD. Minors involve major currencies but exclude USD: EUR/GBP, EUR/JPY, GBP/JPY. Exotics pair a major currency with a smaller/emerging-market currency — often wider spreads and lower liquidity.

WHAT YOU LEARNED

  • Forex means Foreign Exchange, and currencies trade in pairs.
  • The first currency is the base; the second is the quote.
  • Buying generally expects the price to rise; selling expects it to fall.
MODULE 02

Pips

A pip is a standard unit of price movement. For most non-JPY pairs it's commonly the fourth decimal place: 1.1000 → 1.1001 is 1 pip. A move from 1.1000 to 1.1010 is 10 pips.

1.1010 ──────── ▲
                │ 10 pips
1.1000 ──────── ▼

JPY pairs are commonly quoted to two decimals, e.g. USD/JPY 150.00 → 150.01 is usually 1 pip. Always check the symbol's quotation and your broker's contract specs.

Pips and profit/loss

A pip has no single universal cash value — it depends on the currency pair, position size, account currency, exchange rate and contract specs. 10 pips does not automatically mean $10. Some platforms also show an extra decimal called a "point," representing a fractional pip.

WHAT YOU LEARNED

  • A pip measures price movement — usually the 4th decimal (2nd for JPY pairs).
  • Pip value depends on position size and other factors, not a fixed number.
MODULE 03

Bid, Ask and Spread

The Bid is generally the price the market buys your position from you (Sell → Bid). The Ask is generally the price you buy at (Buy → Ask). The difference between them is the spread — a trading cost.

ASK / BUY   1.1002
               ↑ SPREAD (2 pips)
BID / SELL  1.1000

Because you buy at Ask but your position is valued against Bid, a fresh trade can initially show a small unrealized loss roughly equal to the spread, before commissions or price movement. This is normal — it applies to both Buy and Sell positions.

WHAT YOU LEARNED

  • Buys generally execute at Ask; Sells generally execute at Bid.
  • The Bid-Ask difference is the spread, a real cost of trading.
MODULE 04

Forex Brokers

A broker provides the trading account and infrastructure you place trades through — deposits/withdrawals, pricing, execution, MT5 access, support, and account types.

Demo vs live

Demo uses simulated funds — great for learning MT5, practicing orders, and testing your process. Live uses real money, where mistakes have real consequences.

LEARN → PRACTICE → DEMO ACCOUNT → BUILD UNDERSTANDING → ONLY THEN CONSIDER LIVE

What to check before choosing a broker

Regulation by a credible authority, trading costs (spread, commission, overnight swaps), execution quality during volatile markets, account requirements (minimum deposit, position size), and whether MT5 is supported. A broker is a service provider — not a guarantee of profitable trading, no matter what someone online claims.

WHAT YOU LEARNED

  • Demo accounts simulate money; live accounts carry real financial risk.
  • Regulation and costs matter — research a broker before depositing.
MODULE 05

Leverage

Leverage lets a trader control a larger position with a smaller amount of margin — financial amplification. 1:100 leverage means $1 of margin can conceptually provide exposure to up to $100 of position value, subject to broker rules.

A $100 account with 1:100 leverage has a theoretical maximum exposure far larger than the balance — but that does not mean you should use the maximum. That's one of the most common beginner mistakes.

Leverage ≠ risk

This is critical: your actual risk comes from position size, Stop Loss distance, entry price, instrument, volatility, account size and trading costs — not the leverage ratio alone. A trader can have 1:500 leverage and still choose a small, low-risk position. Available leverage is not a target.

WHAT YOU LEARNED

  • Leverage increases potential exposure relative to margin required.
  • Higher leverage doesn't mean higher profit — it magnifies both directions.
MODULE 06

Lot Sizes

A lot describes position size:

Lot sizeCommon name
1.00Standard lot
0.10Mini lot
0.01Micro lot
0.001Nano lot (where supported)

Terminology and minimums vary by broker. The key concept: larger position size = larger monetary effect from the same price movement. If two traders take the same pip move but one uses 0.01 lot and the other 0.10 lot, the larger position sees a larger dollar swing. Never assume "one pip always equals $10" — pip value depends on position size, pair, account currency and current rates.

WHAT YOU LEARNED

  • 1.00 = standard, 0.10 = mini, 0.01 = micro lot.
  • Bigger lots amplify both gains and losses for the same price move.
MODULE 07

Margin, Equity and Balance

Balance = account value from closed trades only. Equity = balance adjusted for unrealized profit/loss on open trades. Margin = funds set aside to support an open leveraged position (not simply "money lost").

Free Margin ≈ Equity − Used Margin  ·  Margin Level ≈ Equity / Used Margin × 100%

If losses reduce equity too far relative to used margin, a broker may issue a margin call, and a stop-out can automatically close positions when margin requirements aren't met. Exact thresholds vary by broker.

WHAT YOU LEARNED

  • Equity = Balance ± unrealized open-trade result.
  • Running out of free margin can trigger a forced close-out.
MODULE 08

Connecting Your Account to MT5

MetaTrader 5 (MT5) is the trading platform you'll use to view markets, open charts, place orders, and manage positions.

  1. Create a trading account (demo or live) with your researched broker.
  2. Install MT5 from an official source or your broker's version.
  3. Open the login screen inside MT5.
  4. Select the correct server (e.g. BrokerName-Demo vs BrokerName-Live) — this matters.
  5. Enter your login number, password and server. Never share your password.
  6. Verify the connection — account info appears, prices update, Market Watch is active.
  7. Open Market Watch to see available instruments (EURUSD, GBPUSD, USDJPY, XAUUSD, etc.)
  8. Open a chart for your chosen pair.

MT5 looks slightly different across Windows, macOS, Android and iOS — menu names and layouts can vary.

WHAT YOU LEARNED

  • The correct server selection matters as much as your login details.
  • Never share your MT5 password with anyone.
MODULE 09

Understanding the MT5 Interface

Market Watch — instruments and current prices. Navigator — accounts, indicators, Expert Advisors, scripts. Charts — price movement; change symbol, timeframe, chart type, indicators. Toolbox/Terminal — open positions, orders, history, alerts. Trade tab — position, volume, symbol, price, SL, TP, profit. History — completed trading activity.

WHAT YOU LEARNED

  • You now know the purpose of Market Watch, Navigator, Charts, Trade tab and History.
MODULE 10

Types of Orders

Two broad categories: market orders (execute now) and pending orders (execute later, if triggered).

OrderGeneral locationBasic idea
Buy MarketCurrent priceBuy now
Sell MarketCurrent priceSell now
Buy LimitBelow current priceBuy lower
Sell LimitAbove current priceSell higher
Buy StopAbove current priceBuy after upward trigger
Sell StopBelow current priceSell after downward trigger

Memory trick: LIMIT = expect a better price / retracement. STOP = enter after price reaches a trigger.

WHAT YOU LEARNED

  • Six core order types: Buy, Sell, Buy/Sell Limit, Buy/Sell Stop.
MODULE 11

Stop Loss

A Stop Loss (SL) closes a position when price hits a specified level, to help control potential loss. For a Buy, SL is generally below entry; for a Sell, generally above entry.

Stop Loss doesn't guarantee exact execution — during extreme volatility, gaps or low liquidity, actual execution can differ from the requested level (slippage). It's a risk-management tool, not a guaranteed loss amount.

Never move your Stop Loss farther away just because price is approaching it — if your original trade idea is invalidated, doing this can increase your risk substantially instead of protecting you.

WHAT YOU LEARNED

  • Buy SL below entry, Sell SL above entry.
  • Moving SL farther away to avoid a loss usually makes things worse.
MODULE 12

Take Profit

A Take Profit (TP) closes a position when a target price is reached, automatically realizing a planned profit.

FeatureStop LossTake Profit
Main purposeLimit potential lossTarget potential profit
Buy positionUsually below entryUsually above entry
Sell positionUsually above entryUsually below entry

WHAT YOU LEARNED

  • SL manages downside; TP defines a target — both can auto-close a trade.
MODULE 13

Break-Even

Moving a Stop Loss to break-even means shifting it toward your entry price so the trade no longer carries its original planned downside — often used to protect an open trade once it's moved favorably.

The danger of moving too early: price can retrace just enough to close the trade at break-even, then continue in your original direction anyway. Moving to break-even is a trade-management decision, not automatically the "safe" choice. Also note: with spread and commission included, closing exactly at your entry price may not equal exactly $0 net.

WHAT YOU LEARNED

  • Break-even reduces downside exposure but can prematurely close a trade that would have worked.
MODULE 14

Your First Complete MT5 Trade

Purely educational example — not a recommendation to buy EUR/USD.

Account Balance$100
PairEUR/USD
DirectionBUY
Position Size0.01 lot
Entry1.1000
Stop Loss1.0980 (20 pips)
Take Profit1.1040 (40 pips)
        TAKE PROFIT  1.1040
                ▲  (40 pips)
        BUY ENTRY    1.1000
                ▼  (20 pips)
        STOP LOSS    1.0980

The core risk lesson: a $100 account does not mean "I should risk $100." Your balance and your risk-per-trade are different concepts. Ask "how much can I afford to lose if I'm wrong?" before asking "how much can I make?"

WHAT YOU LEARNED

  • How a real entry/SL/TP setup fits together, and why account size ≠ risk size.
MODULE 15

Common Beginner Mistakes

  1. Using too much leverage — understand position size and risk before opening a trade.
  2. Oversized lots — too large for the account makes normal moves damaging.
  3. No Stop Loss — "the market will come back" is a hope, not a plan.
  4. Risking too much on one trade — one trade shouldn't decide your account's survival.
  5. Revenge trading — trying to "win back" a loss with an oversized new position.
  6. Overtrading — more trades ≠ more profit.
  7. Trading money you can't afford to lose — never risk essential living expenses.
  8. Not understanding order types before clicking them.
  9. Confusing balance and equity.
  10. Moving Stop Loss farther away as price approaches it.
  11. Going live too quickly — learn MT5 on a demo account first.
